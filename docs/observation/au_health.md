## Possible Research Question(s)

### Has Australia’s high and improving life expectancy concealed a deterioration in deaths from suicide, alcohol and drugs since 2010?

This directly challenges the assumption that “life expectancy alone is a sufficient measure of a country’s health.” This indicator is impressive, easy to grasp intuitively, and closely tied to the question, “How is Australia faring?”

Data plan:
- Australian trends from 2010 onward
- same-year international comparisons
- changes in Australia’s relative position

### Health and social connections

| Social connections | Lack of social support; three-year pooled survey estimate | 4.93% (2008-10) | 10.04% (2023–25) | The proportion lacking support approximately doubled |

Data plan (AI provided):
- Collapse social-support observations into the six independent pooled windows.
- Convert annual harmful-death data into averages matching those windows.
- Plot social support and harmful mortality together for Australia.

Possible conclusion: Longer-lived but less socially supported, with increasing mortality from suicide, alcohol and drugs.

### Health and subjective well-being

Possible RQ: Is Australia’s high life expectancy consistent with Australians’ lived psychological well-being?

| Domain | Measure | Eearlier value | Latest value | Interpretation |
|--------|---------|----------------|--------------|----------------|
| Health | Life expectancy; annual national average | 81.7 years (2010) | 83.0 years (2023) | Living longer overall in Australia |
| Health | Suicide/alcohol/drug deaths; annual deprivation rate | 14.9 per 100,000 (2010) | 22.2 per 100,000 (2024) | Harmful increased |
| Subjective well-being | Negative affect; three-year pooled survey estimate | 12.24% (2008-10) | 14.85% (2023-25) | Negative emotional experiences became more common |
| Subjective well-being | Life satisfaction; sparse survey score | 7.5/10 (2019) | 7.2/10 (2020) | A short-term fall, but insufficient for a long trend |

Data plan (AI provided):
- Use life expectancy to represent objective longevity.
- Use negative affect to represent experienced emotional well-being.
- Use harmful mortality as the severe health outcome.
- Match annual health values to the six independent negative-affect windows.
- Compare Australia’s position and change with peer countries.
- Construct a four-category comparison:long-lived and low negative affect; long-lived and high negative affect; shorter-lived and low negative affect; shorter-lived and high negative affect.
- Examine whether Australia moved between categories since 2010.

Possible conclusion: Australia performs strongly on length of life, but its subjective and harmful-mortality indicators present a less favourable picture.

| Work and job quality | Employment rate; annual percentage, ages 25–64 | 75.4% (2010) | 80.3% (2024) | Employment increased 4.8 percentage points |
| Work and job quality | Employees working long hours; annual deprivation percentage | 14.0% (2010) | 10.2% (2024) | Working hours declined |
| Work and job quality | Gender wage gap; annual/irregular percentage | 14.0% (2010) | 10.7% (2024) | The unadjusted median wage gap narrowed |

Data plan (AI provided):
- Chart the four annual series on aligned panels.
- Standardise each measure to its 2010 value for comparison of direction, without creating a composite score.
- Identify periods of divergence:2010–2016; 2017–2019; 2020–2024.
- Compare Australia with countries reporting all selected indicators.
- Examine whether countries with improving employment or falling long hours also experienced falling harmful mortality.
- Test lagged associations—for example, whether work conditions in one year are associated with mortality one or two years later.
- Conduct a pre/post-2020 sensitivity analysis.

Possible conclusion: Better headline employment conditions did not translate automatically into lower mortality from suicide, alcohol and drugs.
