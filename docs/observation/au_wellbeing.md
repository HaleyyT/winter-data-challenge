Observation:
- Australia's negative affect increased by 2.61 percentage points, or approximately 21.3%, between the first and latest pooled windows.
- The latest negative affect level is 2.46 percentage points above the supplied-country median.
- Negative affect is reported in 16 annual rows, but repeated values within each three-year window are the same estimate. Australia therefore has only six independent periods: 2008-10, 2011-13, 2014-16, 2017-19, 2020-22 and 2023-25.
- Life satisfaction cannot support a long-term trend because Australia has only two observations, in 2019 and 2020.
- The latest negative affect window extends into 2025. It should be labelled clearly if the primary challenge analysis is capped at 2024.

## Possible Research Question(s)

### Has Australia's subjective well-being deteriorated relative to comparable countries since 2010?

This question distinguishes Australia's current position from its direction of change. Australia began close to the supplied-country median for negative affect but was worse than the median in the latest pooled window. The analysis should determine whether this deterioration was unusually large or part of a broader international trend.

Data plan:
- Collapse repeated negative-affect rows into six independent pooled windows.
- Compare Australia's negative affect with the same-window supplied-country median.
- Use Canada, New Zealand, the United Kingdom and the United States as an interpretable peer group, then repeat the analysis using all high-coverage countries and OECD members only.
- Measure both the change in Australia's level and the change in its position relative to peers.
- Use life satisfaction only as supporting evidence for 2019-20, not as a long-term outcome.
- Test whether the conclusion changes after excluding flagged peer observations.

Possible conclusion: Australia appears worse on recent negative affect than it did at the beginning of the period, but the analysis must establish how much of the deterioration is Australia-specific.

Possible issues:
- Negative affect is a survey-based measure, not a diagnosis of mental illness.
- Gallup samples are relatively small and the OECD pools three years to improve precision.
- Cultural and language differences can affect international survey responses.
- Six independent periods are insufficient for complex time-series modelling.
- The supplied-country dataset includes non-OECD countries, so it should not automatically be described as an OECD ranking.

## Possible Relative Fields
### Subjective well-being and social connections

Possible RQ: Has declining social support accompanied the deterioration in Australia's subjective well-being, and is the relationship stronger in Australia than in comparable countries?

| Domain | Measure | Earlier value | Latest value | Interpretation |
|--------|---------|---------------|--------------|----------------|
| Subjective well-being | Negative affect; three-year pooled survey estimate | 12.24% (2008-10) | 14.85% (2023-25) | Negative emotional experiences became more common |
| Subjective well-being | Life satisfaction; sparse survey score | 7.5/10 (2019) | 7.2/10 (2020) | Short-term decline, but insufficient for a long trend |
| Social connections | Lack of social support; three-year pooled survey estimate | 4.93% (2008-10) | 10.04% (2023-25) | The proportion lacking support approximately doubled |

Data plan:
- Collapse both negative affect and lack of social support into their six shared pooled windows.
- Plot the two Australian series and calculate changes between consecutive windows.
- Compare Australia's values and changes with countries reporting both measures.
- Test the cross-country association between lack of support and negative affect within each pooled window.
- Repeat the comparison using all high-coverage countries, OECD members and English-speaking peers.

Possible conclusion: Australia became less socially supported and experienced more negative affect, suggesting a widening social well-being problem.

Possible issues:
- Both indicators come from pooled Gallup survey data and may share sampling or measurement error.
- A national-level association does not show that the same unsupported individuals experienced negative affect.
- The relationship is observational and cannot establish that low social support caused negative affect.
- Repeated annual copies must not be treated as independent observations.

### Subjective well-being and health

Possible RQ: Is Australia's high and improving life expectancy consistent with Australians' lived psychological well-being?

| Domain | Measure | Earlier value | Latest value | Interpretation |
|--------|---------|---------------|--------------|----------------|
| Subjective well-being | Negative affect; three-year pooled survey estimate | 12.24% (2008-10) | 14.85% (2023-25) | Negative emotional experiences became more common |
| Subjective well-being | Life satisfaction; sparse survey score | 7.5/10 (2019) | 7.2/10 (2020) | Short-term decline, but insufficient for a long trend |
| Health | Life expectancy; annual national average | 81.7 years (2010) | 83.0 years (2023) | Australians are living longer overall |
| Health | Suicide/alcohol/drug deaths; annual deprivation rate | 14.9 per 100,000 (2010) | 22.2 per 100,000 (2024) | Harmful mortality increased by 48.9% |

Data plan:
- Use life expectancy to represent objective longevity and negative affect to represent experienced emotional well-being.
- Average annual health observations within the six negative-affect survey windows.
- Compare Australia's objective and subjective health position with same-window peers.
- Examine whether countries that are long-lived also report low negative affect.
- Decompose suicide, alcohol and drug deaths with ABS or AIHW data before making a policy interpretation.

Possible conclusion: Australia performs strongly on length of life, but subjective well-being and harmful mortality present a less favourable picture.

Possible issues:
- Negative affect is not a clinical mental-health measure.
- Life expectancy changes slowly and represents a different health concept.
- The combined harmful-death indicator hides distinct suicide, alcohol and drug trends.
- Cause-of-death coding differs across countries and recent Australian mortality data can be revised.
- Correlation between national averages cannot establish an individual-level relationship.

### Subjective well-being and work/job quality

Possible RQ: Have improvements in employment and working hours been accompanied by better subjective well-being in Australia since 2010?

| Domain | Measure | Earlier value | Latest value | Interpretation |
|--------|---------|---------------|--------------|----------------|
| Subjective well-being | Negative affect; three-year pooled survey estimate | 12.24% (2008-10) | 14.85% (2023-25) | Negative emotional experiences became more common |
| Work and job quality | Employment rate; annual percentage, ages 25-64 | 75.4% (2010) | 80.3% (2024) | Employment increased by 4.8 percentage points |
| Work and job quality | Employees working long hours; annual deprivation percentage | 14.0% (2010) | 10.2% (2024) | Long-hours work declined by 3.8 percentage points |
| Work and job quality | Gender wage gap; annual/irregular percentage | 14.0% (2010) | 10.7% (2024) | The unadjusted median wage gap narrowed |

Data plan:
- Average the annual work indicators within the six negative-affect windows.
- Plot employment, long hours and negative affect on aligned panels rather than combining them into one score.
- Identify periods when work indicators and negative affect moved in opposite directions.
- Compare Australia with countries reporting all selected indicators in the same periods.
- Repeat the analysis before and after 2020 and test one-window lags as a sensitivity check.

Possible conclusion: Improvements in headline employment conditions did not automatically translate into lower negative affect.

Possible issues:
- Employment of at least one paid hour does not measure job security, pay adequacy, underemployment or workplace stress.
- National averages conceal differences by occupation, sex, age and income.
- Long-hours definitions may differ across countries.
- With only six well-being periods, lagged results will be exploratory and statistically weak.
- Other economic, social and pandemic-related factors may explain the divergence.

### Subjective well-being and income/wealth

Possible RQ: Has Australia's rising material prosperity translated into better subjective well-being since 2010?

| Domain | Measure | Earlier value | Latest value | Interpretation |
|--------|---------|---------------|--------------|----------------|
| Subjective well-being | Negative affect; three-year pooled survey estimate | 12.24% (2008-10) | 14.85% (2023-25) | Negative emotional experiences became more common |
| Income and wealth | Adjusted disposable income per capita; annual real PPP amount | US$44,625 (2010) | US$50,629 (2024) | Average material resources increased by 13.5% |
| Income and wealth | S80/S20 income ratio; periodic inequality measure | 5.52 (2012) | 5.56 (2020) | Little evidence that income inequality improved |
| Income and wealth | Median net wealth; periodic household measure | US$347,898 (2012) | US$369,820 (2020) | Median wealth increased by approximately 6.3% |

Data plan:
- Match annual income observations to the six negative-affect windows.
- Compare growth in material resources with changes in negative affect.
- Examine whether Australia's income and well-being relationship differs from that of peer countries.
- Separate average prosperity from distribution by including the S80/S20 ratio where common years are available.
- Use levels, changes and same-period peer ranks; do not construct an arbitrarily weighted prosperity score.

Possible conclusion: Australia became materially wealthier while negative affect increased, so income growth alone did not guarantee improved subjective well-being.

Possible issues:
- Adjusted disposable income is a national-accounts measure, not household cash income or wages.
- Inequality and wealth observations are sparse and end earlier than the main income series.
- The data cannot connect an individual's income or wealth with their well-being.
- Cross-country relationships are confounded by institutions, culture, demographics and public services.
- The analysis supports association and divergence, not a causal claim about income and well-being.

## Recommended Direction

The strongest two-domain question is:

> Has declining social support accompanied the deterioration in Australia's subjective well-being, and is this relationship stronger in Australia than in comparable countries?

This direction is recommended because the two indicators share aligned pooled windows, have complete 47-country coverage, and create a focused story about social experience. The health pairing is the strongest alternative if the aim is to contrast Australia's strong objective outcomes with weaker lived outcomes.
