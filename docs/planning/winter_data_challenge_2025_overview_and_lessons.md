# Winter Data Analysis Challenge 2025 — Overview and Lessons

Link: https://spds.sydney.edu.au/winter-data-analysis-challenge-2025/

## Challenge Overview

The **2025 Winter Data Analysis Challenge** was the third annual challenge hosted by the University of Sydney's data science community.

The challenge gives undergraduate students an opportunity to:

- work with a complex, real-world dataset;
- demonstrate creativity and analytical skill;
- integrate the supplied data with large open datasets;
- generate meaningful and context-aware insights;
- communicate findings through a reproducible report and short presentation.

Submissions were judged according to:

1. **Analytical innovation**
2. **Clarity of communication**
3. **Critical thinking**
4. **Contextual understanding**

Prizes were awarded for first, second, and third place, with additional honourable mentions recognising other outstanding submissions.

---

## 2025 Dataset

Participants worked with **New South Wales Government Transport Open Data**.

The supplied information included:

- Sydney train station entries and exits;
- Opal card patronage data;
- observations covering January 2020 to July 2025.

Participants were encouraged to explore how people move through Sydney and identify factors that influence travel behaviour.

This dataset supported many possible directions, including:

- station accessibility;
- passenger facilities;
- public transport policy;
- commuting patterns;
- changes in work behaviour;
- transport privatisation;
- geographic inequality;
- the impact of major events;
- differences between weekdays and weekends;
- changes before, during, and after the COVID-19 period.

## What the 2025 Results Suggest

The winning projects show that successful challenge entries are often built around a **specific real-world question**, not around a particular algorithm.

The topics focused on:

- infrastructure and accessibility;
- public policy and privatisation;
- behavioural change in commuting.

These are broader than questions such as:

- “Which station has the highest patronage?”
- “Can we predict passenger volume?”
- “Which model has the best accuracy?”

The winning questions connect data analysis to systems that affect people and policy.

---

## Patterns Across the Winning Topics

### 1. Clear and Focused Questions

Each project appears to have a recognisable central question or argument.

This gives the analysis direction and helps the audience understand why each step matters.

### 2. Wider Context

The projects connect transport data with:

- accessibility;
- customer facilities;
- public ownership;
- commuting culture;
- changing work patterns.

This closely matches the challenge's emphasis on contextual understanding.

### 3. Opportunity for External Data

The topics likely required or benefited from external information such as:

- station facility records;
- accessibility information;
- bus route ownership or contract data;
- geographic boundaries;
- employment or work-from-home trends;
- policy announcements;
- census information.

### 4. Strong Storytelling Potential

The project titles communicate an idea, not just a technique.

For example:

- *The Last Public Routes*
- *The Fracturing of the 9-to-5 Commute*

These titles create curiosity and immediately suggest a narrative.

### 5. Analysis Beyond a Single Metric

The topics invite:

- maps;
- timelines;
- comparisons;
- statistical models;
- visual explanations;
- policy discussion;
- sensitivity analysis;
- limitations.

This supports the judges' preference for data analysis beyond a single performance score.

## Lessons for 2026 Participants

### Choose a Question with Real-World Meaning

A technically correct analysis may still feel weak if the research question is unimportant or overly broad.

Look for a question that:

- reveals something not immediately obvious;
- matters to a real group of people;
- connects to a broader system or policy;
- can be investigated convincingly with the available data.

### Use External Data to Explain, Not Decorate

External datasets should help answer the main question.

Possible purposes include:

- adding demographic context;
- measuring accessibility;
- identifying policy changes;
- controlling for weather or geography;
- comparing regions;
- explaining anomalies.

### Develop a Narrative

A strong project should be explainable as a sequence:

1. We noticed a meaningful problem.
2. We formed a focused question.
3. We selected relevant data.
4. We found an initial pattern.
5. We investigated why it occurred.
6. We tested whether the result was reliable.
7. We reached a careful conclusion.
8. We explained why the conclusion matters.

### Use Memorable Titles

A good title should communicate the project's central idea.

#### Weak title

> Analysis of Transport Data

#### Stronger title

> Beyond the CBD: How Hybrid Work Reshaped Sydney's Commuter Peaks

A strong title is:

- specific;
- concise;
- understandable;
- connected to the main finding or question.

## Key Takeaway

The 2025 winners demonstrate that the Winter Data Analysis Challenge rewards teams that do more than describe a dataset.

A standout submission:

- asks a meaningful question;
- investigates it rigorously;
- integrates relevant external context;
- follows up on initial findings;
- critically evaluates the evidence;
- communicates the result as a compelling story.
