# Is Australia doing well? Initial evidence and question direction

## Short answer

Australia looks **materially stronger than in 2010, but the improvement is uneven**. Household income and employment rose, the gender wage gap narrowed and fewer employees worked very long hours. Yet income inequality was essentially flat, the national housing-affordability average did not improve, and Australians reporting a lack of social support roughly doubled across OECD pooled survey windows. Negative affect also worsened. Australia therefore looks stronger on material position than on its recent social direction.

This is a scouting conclusion, not a causal claim. It is based on same-year comparisons, direction of change and the OECD definitions; no composite score or arbitrary weights were used.

## How the exploration was conducted

1. Preserve the raw file and audit country-indicator-year coverage.
2. Cap the primary period at 2024 to match the challenge brief.
3. Collapse repeated Gallup rows into independent 3-year windows.
4. Compare Australia only with countries reporting the same indicator in the same year.
5. Examine both current position and direction since the first Australian observation.
6. Use Canada, New Zealand, the United Kingdom and the United States as an interpretable English-speaking sensitivity group, not as the only definition of peers.
7. Treat thin, sparse or structurally non-comparable indicators as supporting context rather than headline evidence.

## Meaningful patterns

### 1. Material resources improved, especially income and employment

- Real PPP-adjusted household income per capita rose from **USD 44,625 in 2010 to USD 50,629 in 2024 (+13.5%)**. Australia was above the 2024 supplied-country median of USD 42,721 (32 countries) and the English-speaking peer median of USD 46,176 (three reporting peers).
- Employment among people aged 25-64 increased from **75.4% to 80.3% (+4.8 percentage points)**. Australia was close to the 2024 supplied-country median (79.9%) and English-speaking peer median (79.7%).
- Income peaked at USD 52,430 in 2021 and was 3.4% lower by 2024. The long-run gain is real, but “continuously improving” would be misleading.

**Why it matters:** Australia became materially better resourced, but its latest position is stronger for income than for employment, and the post-2021 income retreat deserves acknowledgement.

### 2. Some labour inequality improved, but broad sharing is not established

- The gender wage gap fell from **14.0% in 2010 to 10.7% in 2024**, a 3.4-point improvement. The latest value equals the supplied-country median and is below the four-peer median of 14.8%.
- The S80/S20 income ratio was **5.52 in 2012 and 5.56 in 2020**: essentially no progress, with a slight unfavourable movement. Australia was a little more unequal than the 2020 supplied-country median of 5.17, though slightly better than the English-speaking peer median of 5.68.
- Median household wealth rose 6.3% from 2012 to 2020 and was high among the nine countries reporting in 2020, but that comparison is too thin and the measure excludes pensions.

**Why it matters:** higher average resources do not by themselves show that gains were broadly shared. The supplied file supports a focused inequality analysis, but not a complete account of Australian inequality.

### 3. Work conditions improved, but Australia still has a long-hours problem

- Employees working long paid hours fell from **14.0% in 2010 to 10.2% in 2024**, a substantial improvement.
- Despite that progress, the 2024 supplied-country median was only **3.9%** and the English-speaking peer median was 9.2%. Australia sat around the **20th favourable percentile** among 41 reporting countries.
- The OECD housing-affordability average was almost unchanged: **80.76% of adjusted disposable income remained after housing costs in 2010 versus 80.75% in 2024**.

**Why it matters:** direction and position tell different stories. Australia is improving on long hours but remains weak internationally. The housing average should not be used to dismiss the housing crisis because it hides distribution and includes imputed owner-occupier rent.

### 4. Social support deteriorated, but much of the decline is shared by peers

- Lack of social support increased from **4.93% in the 2008-10 pooled window to 10.04% in 2023-25**. This is more than double, but it represents six independent windows, not 16 annual observations.
- The English-speaking peer median also worsened, from **4.84% to 9.25%**. The latest supplied-country median was 9.41% (47 countries). Australia is slightly worse, but the broad deterioration is not uniquely Australian.
- Negative affect rose from **12.24% to 14.85%** across the same pooled-window endpoints. The latest supplied-country median was 12.39%, placing Australia around the 26th favourable percentile; the English-speaking peer median rose from 10.87% to 12.88%.

**Why it matters:** the strongest social result is a shared deterioration with Australia somewhat worse at the latest endpoint. The follow-up question should ask whether Australia diverged from a general OECD-era trend, rather than presenting the entire change as Australia-specific.

### 5. Health and skills provide important counter-signals

- Life expectancy increased from **81.7 years in 2010 to 83.0 in 2023**, above the same-year supplied-country median of 81.5.
- Deaths from suicide, alcohol and drugs rose from **14.9 to 22.2 per 100,000 (+48.9%)** by 2024. Only 18 countries reported in 2024 and the combined cause measure requires decomposition before drawing policy conclusions.
- PISA mathematics fell from **504 points in 2012 to 487 in 2022**, while remaining above the 2022 supplied-country median of 479.

**Why it matters:** “healthy” and “skilled” depend on whether current level or direction is prioritised. Australia remains comparatively strong in life expectancy and mathematics, while recent direction is less reassuring.

## Best-scoped research direction

### Recommended main question

> **Has Australia's material progress since 2010 coincided with better social well-being, or has a gap opened relative to comparable countries?**

This is stronger than four separate domain reports because it builds one memorable tension: improving material conditions versus weakening social experience.

Use three tightly connected subquestions:

1. **Material progress:** Did household income and employment improve relative to same-year peers?
2. **Breadth:** Were improvements accompanied by lower income/wage inequality and fewer long working hours?
3. **Lived outcome:** Did social support and negative affect improve, and was Australia's change unusual among peers?

Primary indicators should be fixed before deeper modelling: income, employment, S80/S20 inequality, gender wage gap, long hours, lack of social support and negative affect. Housing affordability can be a caveat or external-data motivation rather than a primary outcome.

### Other viable questions

| Question | Strength | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| Has Australia's material progress since 2010 been broadly shared? | Clean economic story using income, employment, wage gap and S80/S20 | Supplied inequality data are sparse and omit within-group detail |
| Is Australia healthy, or merely long-lived? | Strong contrast between life expectancy and preventable-harm deaths | Latest death comparison has only 18 countries and combines three causes |
| Is Australia socially connected despite high civic participation? | Memorable contrast between social support, political voice and turnout | Political voice has two waves; turnout is distorted by compulsory voting |

The first alternative is safest. The recommended material-social question is more distinctive if the team can handle pooled windows and peer sensitivity clearly.

## Analysis path after question selection

1. Write a short protocol fixing the period, peer groups, indicators, directions and evidence that would weaken the claim.
2. Show a **current-position versus change** chart so “high but declining” is distinguishable from “low but improving”.
3. For annual economic measures, estimate simple slopes with uncertainty and show raw trends first.
4. For social indicators, analyse the six pooled windows once each; never regress on repeated annual copies.
5. Test whether Australia's change relative to the peer median widens, and repeat with all high-coverage countries.
6. Run robustness checks: alternative peer groups, pre-2020 versus full period, exclusion of flagged values, leave-one-peer-out results, and levels versus ranks.
7. Use external data only to close a defined gap. ABS income distribution or housing stress by income group would deepen “broadly shared”; disaggregated mortality would clarify the health signal.

Avoid a single weighted “Australia score”, causal language, synthetic data augmentation, and increasingly complex models without a specific analytical purpose.

# Health Aspects -- Is AUS healthy or just living longer?

Australia’s health status is mixed. On the surface, Australia looks healthy: life expectancy increased from 81.7 years in 2010 to 83.0 years in 2023, placing Australia among the stronger-performing countries. 
This suggests that Australians are, broadly, living longer and that Australia performs well on a traditional health outcome.
However, deaths from suicide, alcohol, and drugs had sharp rise 2010–2016, then plateau at a high level. 
From 14.9 deaths per 100,000 people in 2010 to 22.2 in 2024.  

This indicates that while Australians are living longer overall, preventable deaths linked to mental health and substance use have worsened.
This might to be connected to Australia’s lack of social support which increased from 4.9% in 2010 to 10.0% in 2024.

Compared with similar countries, Australia looks strong on life expectancy. 
It’s close to Japan, Korea, Sweden, and France, and performs better than New Zealand, Canada, the UK, and the US.
But on deaths from suicide, alcohol, and drugs, Australia is more middle-ranked. 
It performs better than the UK, Korea, Canada, and the US, but worse than the Netherlands, France, Japan, and Sweden.

The health findings fit well with the existing story that Australia is stronger on headline/material indicators, 
but weaker on lived social well-being indicators.
